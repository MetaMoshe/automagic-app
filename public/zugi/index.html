Section 01

Summary

In 2023, AI could write fluent text but could not produce investment analysis that a professional investor would rely on. Zugi.ai asked whether it could, and Automagic built the answer from the ground up. We designed and built a hybrid reasoning platform that combines fixed, explainable scoring with large language models, grounds every answer in real company data and research, and remembers how each investor likes to work, without retraining a model.

The problem. Private investing runs on closed networks, expensive tools and gut feel. Angels, syndicates and first-time investors have almost no tools at all. Generic AI made this worse in one way: its answers sounded right but were not consistent, repeatable or traceable, so investors spent hours checking them.

The solution. An orchestration-first platform. Software decides what each request needs, gathers company data, live research and the user's own investment profile, and passes that context to the AI. Core investment scores stay deterministic. The AI explains them, interprets findings and writes the analysis in the order an analyst would write an investment memo.

The pivot. In 2025 the foundation-model providers released features that overlapped much of Zugi.ai's context, history and agent work, and users were wary of how their data was used. In a six-month rebuild we moved the platform from a proprietary small language model to a multi-LLM vertical AI architecture, added a governance layer for data privacy, and wired live client feedback into the prompts.

The result. A working platform with more than 200 design partners across four regions, moving from beta into go-to-market on a freemium-to-premium model. Because each layer could be replaced on its own, the platform survived a major market shift without starting again.

Section 02

The client

Zugi.ai (Zugi Ventures Ltd, United Kingdom) is an AI-native investment intelligence platform for private-market investors: venture capital, private equity, family offices, angel investors and syndicates. It calls its category Aii, Artificial Investment Intelligence, and set out to build the reasoning layer behind investment conviction.

The business aim went beyond the technology. If the platform could give trustworthy, explainable insight to anyone regardless of experience, newer and smaller investors could take part in private markets with confidence. Zugi.ai calls this VIBE investing: Vision, Insight, Belief and Execution.

Section 04

The technical problem

We were not trying to invent a new large language model. The question was whether existing models could be orchestrated in a different way, so they performed reliable investment reasoning instead of generating plausible text. Nobody knew at the outset whether that was possible. It broke down into six technical unknowns, all part of one objective.

#UnknownWhy existing approaches fell short in 2023
T1Reasoning fidelity: can AI reason through a deal like an analyst, and explain why?Models produced fluent text, but there was no established way to make them give consistent, repeatable, explainable analysis across companies and sectors
T2Symbolic logic: can fixed rules and scoring guide the AI?AI systems relied on probabilistic generation; there was no proven way to combine deterministic scoring and AI reasoning in one workflow
T3Domain reasoning: can a general model think like a professional investor without retraining?General models lacked VC and PE judgement, and there was no proven way to add it through orchestration alone
T4Data fusion: can structured databases and free text feed one reasoning process?Financial databases and pitch decks, websites and news use different formats and were analysed separately
T5Personalisation: can the platform remember how each investor works?Most AI systems had little persistent memory; changing behaviour meant editing prompts by hand or retraining
T6Auditability: can scores update as facts change, with a record of why?Generated answers changed without explanation, which made them impossible to review

Section 05

The solution

We built a hybrid reasoning platform. Deterministic software controls the workflow, the data and the scores. Language models are used only where reasoning and language add value: interpreting findings, explaining scores, personalising insight and writing the analysis. Seven components do the work.

1 · Request orchestrator

Requests never go straight to a model. The orchestrator works out the user's intent, the data required, the external sources to call, the analytical workflow to run and the shape of the response. B1 T1

2 · Context builder and data fusion

Data pipelines take structured company data (Crunchbase), live web research (Perplexity), internal knowledge and the user's profile, normalise them, and assemble one grounded context for each request. Answers rest on real information, not only on what the model remembers. T4 B5

3 · Deterministic scoring engine

Core investment scores are calculated by fixed, user-configurable formulas, not by the AI. The scores become structured inputs to the reasoning step, so the AI reasons from defined signals. When new information arrives the score is recalculated and the reason for the change is kept. T2 T6 B4

4 · AI reasoning layer

Structured prompts, reusable templates and domain-specific reasoning sequences guide general models to work through a company the way an analyst would, and to explain the conclusion in investment-memo order. Outputs are returned as JSON blocks, so the interface can lay them out consistently. T1 T3

5 · Agentic workflow

Instead of one large prompt, the platform runs specialised AI tasks for research, company analysis, investor profiling, investment tips, formula explanation and report generation. Application logic sets the order and routing, and the output of one task feeds the next. B1 B2

6 · Two kinds of memory

Conversational memory keeps continuity within a conversation. User memory stores each investor's preferences, scoring formulas, custom weights, investment profile and history. Personalisation works through stored memory and adaptive weighting, with the underlying model unchanged. T5 B3

7 · Feedback loops and governance

Users refine their criteria and weights, and future analysis follows. After the pivot, live client feedback flows into the training prompts, and a governance layer controls how agents access, share and keep data. M1 M2

Section 06

The product

Users worked in one interface. The left-hand menu grouped the AI agents (ZugiGPT, Co-Pilot, VC Matrix and Masters Board) and the Datahub (Search Companies, Find Investors, People Lookup). Quick actions started common jobs: find deals, generate a memo, ask ZugiGPT, or mirror how top-tier investors think. While an answer was prepared, the user saw each step and the sources used.

Assess this seed-stage fintech against my thesis and draft a short memo.

    Intent is a company assessment plus a memo. The workflow and required sources are chosen.

  2. 02

  3. 03

  4. 04

    Structured blocks appear in the interface with sources. The user's adjustments feed back into memory.

Section 07

Architecture

The platform followed an orchestration-first design. Data retrieval, business logic, AI reasoning and presentation were built as separate layers, so each could be maintained or replaced on its own. That decision is what later made the pivot possible in six months.

External data providers and AI services sat behind service layers, which kept them loosely coupled. Adding a data source or switching a model meant changing one service, not the workflow around it.

Section 09

The pivot: from SLM to multi-LLM vertical AI

About two years after the work began, the foundation-model providers launched their own offerings for financial services. Much of what had made Zugi.ai different (context, chat history and agentic features) was suddenly available out of the box. At the same time, users were asking hard questions about their data. We led a six-month discovery, redesign and rebuild in response.

#ProblemEffect
M1Model releases matched Zugi.ai's context, history and agent featuresThe moat built on those features was at risk
M2Users distrusted how their data was handled and whether it trained the underlying modelFirms held back sensitive deal data, which limited adoption

What we did

  1. Discovery: moat and trust review. Mapped where new model releases overlapped the platform, and what users needed before they would trust the agent layer with their data.
  2. Design: vertical AI strategy and architecture. Moved the strategy from a proprietary small language model to a multi-LLM vertical platform for private markets, with governance designed in from the start.
  3. Build: platform rebuild. Built the governance layer, moved context and chat history onto leading LLMs, and connected live client feedback loops to the training prompts.

Before and after

AreaBeforeAfter
Model layerProprietary vertical small language modelSeveral leading LLMs, used flexibly by task
Context and historyCustom built, competing with model providersBuilt on the providers' own context and chat history, so effort went into what was unique
Data handlingUsers unsure where their data wentA governance layer controls how agents access, share and keep data; what users share is never passed on or used to train the underlying model
LearningDesign-partner feedback through community groups and manual prompt workLive client feedback and real workflows feed straight into the prompts
PositionAn AI co-pilot that new model releases could copyA full vertical AI platform for private markets

The layered architecture meant the model layer could be swapped without rewriting the data pipelines, the scoring engine or the interface.

Section 11

How we worked

Development followed an iterative product discovery cycle. The aim was to test assumptions early, before adding functionality.

  1. Business problem framed in investor terms
  2. Customer discovery through interviews with investors and design partners
  3. Requirements written as PRDs and prioritised on a roadmap
  4. Architecture design with the layers kept separate
  5. Rapid prototype of the smallest useful workflow
  6. Internal testing of software and AI output
  7. User feedback from beta users and design partners
  8. Iteration and release, with the backlog refined continuously

AI development

Prompt engineering evolved in steps: structured prompts, reusable templates, JSON outputs and modular AI tasks, each aimed at fewer hallucinations and more consistent results.

Software engineering

  • GitHub version control
  • Modular Python architecture and API-first design
  • Reusable components and separation of concerns

Section 12

Testing and validation

Testing combined standard software testing with checks designed for AI output. The hardest question was whether the reasoning was sound, so we tested it where the answer was already known.

TypeWhat we checked
FunctionalAPI tests, workflow validation, integration and regression testing
AI evaluationFactual accuracy, consistency, reasoning quality, structured output and prompt reliability. Prompts were iterated until quality was acceptable
Back-testingHistorical investment cases with known outcomes were run through the platform, to see whether it picked up the right signals, reached sound conclusions and avoided hindsight bias. Simulated investments tested it further
User testingBeta users rated usability, response quality, usefulness for investing and workflow speed, and their feedback set feature priorities
